# The five principles of customer acquisition: from marketing theory to practice (knowledge provided by the user, 2026-10-18)

## Conclusion
Customer acquisition is not gathering people through ads or sales. It is "delivering the right value, at the right place and time, for a problem a specific customer has, and building customer relationships that generate profit." This is consistent with Kotler's marketing theory.
Because AI is lowering the cost of development, research, ad production and sales preparation, run it in this order:
Market research → customer selection → problem identification → value proposition → validating acquisition channels → sales → continuous improvement
Do not build a product and then look for customers. First discover the conditions under which acquisition and purchase work, then build the product.

Theoretical foundations:
| Theory / proponent | Core idea | Use here |
|---|---|---|
| Philip Kotler | STP, 4P, customer value | Choosing markets and customers |
| Theodore Levitt | Marketing myopia | Think from customer wants, not the product |
| Clayton Christensen et al. | Jobs to Be Done | Identify the job the customer wants done |
| Michael Porter | Competitive strategy, differentiation | Designing an advantage over competitors |
| Byron Sharp | Mental and physical availability | Being recalled by customers and available to buy |
| Peter Fader et al. | Customer value, customer-centric management | Thinking about profitability per customer |
| Eric Ries | Lean Startup | Testing hypotheses with small experiments |

The five principles: 1. Who — whom to sell to / 2. What — what to solve / 3. Where — where to acquire customers / 4. Why — why customers choose us over competitors / 5. How — how to turn it into repeatable profit.
Each principle is organised in the order "theory → internet research → how to judge → how to experiment".

## Principle 1: Who — whom to sell to
### Theory: Kotler's STP
- Segmentation: divide the market into groups of customers with shared characteristics.
- Targeting: choose the customer group to win first.
- Positioning: design what value the company stands for in the minds of the chosen customers.
Do not start by targeting "as many people as possible". Example: "a sales support tool for Japanese companies" is too broad. "Managers at IT companies with 20–100 employees who handle new B2B sales and spend 5+ hours a week building prospect lists" is specific enough to actually find customers.

Five axes for segmenting customers:
| Axis | What to check |
|---|---|
| Attribute | Industry, company size, location |
| Situation | Expanding hiring, strengthening sales, launching a business |
| Behavior | Use of specific software, job posts, advertising |
| Problem | Lack of time, rising costs, lost sales opportunities |
| Purchase conditions | Budget, decision maker, timing of adoption, existing contracts |
In B2B, the situation a company is in right now matters as much as its attributes (a company that just launched a new business buys different things from one maintaining an existing business).

### Internet research
Official company websites, careers pages, press releases, annual securities reports, job boards, case studies of existing services. Fields to structure with AI: company / industry / employees / situation (e.g. hiring 3 B2B sales reps) / inferred problem / evidence of the problem (job post URL) / likely buyer (head of sales) / reach path (official contact form).
A job post lets you infer possible sales expansion, but you cannot conclude they struggle with building prospect lists. Store the observed facts and the inferred problem separately.

### How to judge (more important than total market size)
- Target companies can be identified concretely
- A certain number of companies share the same problem
- The person in charge of purchasing can be identified
- They have the ability to pay
- They can be contacted repeatedly with the same method
Even if there are only a few thousand target companies, the market can be attractive if price and margin are high enough.

### How to experiment
Extract about 100 companies from a specific segment and approach them individually. Measure not just the reply rate but target companies → rate of reaching the person in charge → meeting rate → problem-match rate → purchase intent.

Done when: you can build a list of customer companies and explain why those companies might buy.

## Principle 2: What — what to solve
### Theory: Levitt and Jobs to Be Done
Marketing myopia = focusing so much on your own product that you lose sight of the value the customer really wants. What customers want is not the sales list itself but more meetings and sales.
JTBD example: surface need "I want to build a sales list" → actual job "a new-business rep wants to find companies to approach this week" → deeper purpose "get more meetings out of a limited sales team". Broken down this far, prioritising prospects, identifying the responsible department, and notifying the right timing to approach may all become value.

### Five elements of demand strength
| Item | What to evaluate |
|---|---|
| Severity | How much trouble it causes if unsolved |
| Frequency | How often it recurs |
| Economic loss | Size of the loss in money, time and opportunity |
| Urgency | By when it must be solved |
| Existing spending | Whether they already spend money to solve it |
Even a low-frequency problem is attractive if a single loss is large (daily data entry vs. an important annual legal compliance task). Do not use a simple total score; also distinguish the type of problem.

### Strength of evidence
| Evidence | What it shows | Reliability |
|---|---|---|
| Actual contracts or orders | Money is being spent | High |
| Paid use of existing products | The market is willing to pay | High |
| Budgeted calls for proposals or tenders | Concrete intent to buy | High |
| Competitors' case studies | Track record of spending to solve the problem | Fairly high |
| Complaints in product reviews | Room for improvement | Medium |
| Search demand | People are looking for information | Medium |
| Complaints on social media | The problem may exist | Low to medium |
| AI inference about the problem | A hypothesis to research | Low |
Prioritise markets where existing spending and complaints are confirmed at the same time. However, even with complaints, entry is hard if switching costs are high.

### Quantifying the problem
Example: 40 hours a month building sales lists × ¥4,000/hour labour cost = ¥160,000 a month. An 80% reduction is ¥128,000 in theory. But this is monetised time saved; it does not necessarily reduce cash spending. Convert the problem into money, time and sales opportunities.

Done when: you can explain, with evidence, who struggles with what in which situation, how much they currently bear, and what improves when it is solved.

## Principle 3: Where — where to acquire customers
### Theory: the 4Ps and Byron Sharp
| 4P | Meaning | Relation to acquisition |
|---|---|---|
| Product | Product or service | The value customers want |
| Price | Price | The economic barrier to purchase |
| Place | Distribution and sales channels | Where customers can buy |
| Promotion | Promotion | How to get noticed and considered |
Acquisition is not only Promotion (if you are built into tools customers use every day, contact happens without ads).
Sharp: Mental Availability (easy to recall at the moment of considering a purchase) / Physical Availability (easy to find and buy). What matters is not awareness but being recalled at the buying moment and actually being purchasable.
Applied example (a tender information service): appearing in search results when someone thinks "I want to find tenders" = the point of recall and discovery. If they can view tender details straight from the search result and sign up free right there, the barrier drops. Design a state where customers reach your service at the very moment they feel the problem.

### Principles for choosing acquisition channels
| Customer state | Suitable channel | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Searching for a solution | SEO, search ads | "sales list building tool" |
| Comparing existing products | Comparison pages, reviews | "alternatives to X" |
| Has the problem but is not looking | Direct sales | Proposals to specific companies |
| Gathers information from peers | Referrals, communities | Industry associations, experts |
| The problem arises during work | Free tools, integrations | Free company search |
| Checks information regularly | Notifications, email | Delivery of new tenders |
Where customers gather and where purchase intent arises are different (even if many executives are on social media, they will not necessarily buy corporate insurance there. A proposal reaching a company close to its renewal date can turn into a meeting with few contacts).

### Research methods
Search path: Google search results, Keyword Planner, Search Console, your own search data. Fields: search keyword / search volume / search intent (information gathering or purchase consideration) / competing pages / ads shown / structure of the search results (could a new site enter). Do not judge by search volume alone (B2B keywords with clear purchase intent and high prices are valuable even with low volume).
Direct sales path: extract companies with a high likelihood of purchase from official websites, job posts, press releases and business reports. Do not collect indiscriminately; detect events that trigger purchases (triggers): mass hiring of sales staff / establishing a new division / opening a new office / calls for outsourcing a specific operation / planned renewal of an existing system. They do not guarantee purchase but are strong hypotheses for narrowing sales targets.

### Five axes for evaluating acquisition channels
| Axis | Criterion |
|---|---|
| Reachability | Can you contact the customer |
| Purchase intent | Is the problem recognised at the time of contact |
| Acquisition cost | How much it costs to acquire one customer |
| Repeatability | Can you keep acquiring with the same method |
| Scalability | Can you increase acquisitions by increasing investment |

Done when: you can explain where customers are, at what timing, and through which channel to contact them, and you can run a small acquisition experiment.

## Principle 4: Why — why customers choose us
### Theory: Porter's competitive strategy
Competitive advantage comes mainly from cost advantage and differentiation. Lower development and operating costs through AI may become an advantage, but being cheap to build with AI is not a long-term differentiator in itself (competitors can use AI too). Merely reproducing an existing service cheaply leads to price competition. Use the low-cost structure to create differences that mean something to customers.

### Reasons to switch
Switching has costs: data migration, training, process change, security review.
Conceptual formula: expected value of the new service − adoption burden − switching risk > value of the status quo
Example: replacing ¥100,000/month with ¥30,000/month saves ¥840,000 a year, but if migration costs ¥1,000,000 and operational risk is high, they will not switch right away. If it can be used alongside the existing service with almost no setup work, it is more likely to be adopted.

### Types of differentiation
| Differentiation | Example | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Price | Much cheaper than competitors | Easy to imitate |
| Performance | Higher accuracy or speed | Needs objective verification |
| Convenience | Easy to adopt and operate | Needs continuous UX improvement |
| Expertise | Optimised for a specific industry | The target market becomes narrow |
| Data | Owns unique information | Collection and update costs are needed |
| Distribution | Customers find it naturally | An acquisition base must be built |
| Trust | Quality assurance, track record, certification | Takes time to earn |
Pay particular attention to combinations of "price", "data" and "distribution". A free company database will be copied if it is only free, but it becomes hard to imitate once continuously collected data, users' search conditions, proprietary change detection and integrations with related services accumulate. Data volume alone is not an advantage, though (accuracy, uniqueness, usage rights, update frequency and value to customers are needed).

### Research methods (do not compare feature lists only; research the conditions that decide purchases)
| Research target | What to check |
|---|---|
| Positive reviews | The value customers rate most highly |
| Negative reviews | Complaints and unsolved problems |
| Posts about cancelling | Reasons for leaving |
| Competitor comparison articles | Criteria used when purchasing |
| Pricing | Relationship between price and value delivered |
| Case studies | Reasons for adoption and effects |
Question: what is the clear reason a customer would stop using the existing service, or newly add ours? If this is vague, it should not be built.

Done when: you can explain in one sentence why a specific customer chooses our service, and that reason is backed by customers' actual reviews or behavior.

## Principle 5: How — how to make profitable acquisition repeatable
### Theory: customer lifetime value and unit economics
CAC = cost of acquiring customers ÷ number of new paying customers (example: ¥200,000 in ads + ¥100,000 in sales for 10 companies → ¥30,000. Keep consistent how labour and production costs are included).
LTV ≈ monthly revenue × contribution margin × average months retained (example: ¥30,000/month × 80% × 24 months = ¥576,000). In a new business, retention is not yet known, so assume several retention periods and run a sensitivity analysis.

### Breaking down the funnel (B2B direct sales)
| Stage | Metric |
|---|---|
| Extracting target companies | Number of companies matching the criteria |
| Contact | Number of companies contacted |
| Response | Reply / inquiry rate |
| Meeting | Meeting conversion rate |
| Proposal | Proposal rate |
| Contract | Close rate |
| Retention | Retention rate |
| Expansion | Upsell / referral rate |
Example: 1,000 companies → 50 responses → 20 meetings → 5 contracts gives a 0.5% contract rate from the targets. Do not just increase volume; identify which stage is the bottleneck. Low response rate → targeting or messaging. High meeting rate but low close rate → price, features, trust or adoption burden.

### The AI improvement cycle
Data collection (ads, search, inquiries, meetings, usage) → cause analysis (changes in acquisition, close and retention rates) → improvement hypotheses (target, messaging, price, features, flow) → experiments (changes to LP, ad copy, onboarding) → evaluation (impact on revenue, contribution margin, CAC).
An AI proposing improvements does not mean they worked. Comparisons such as A/B tests or staged rollouts are needed. Early on there may be no significant difference, so combine quantitative data with interviews to judge.

Done when: you can measure the key metrics from acquiring new customers to continued use, and there is a prospect of increasing acquisition while maintaining profit.

## A business exploration process integrating the five principles
| Step | Principle | What to confirm by research | What to confirm by experiment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who | Target customers, number of companies, buyer | Can we actually contact them |
| 2 | What | Problem, existing spending, complaints | Do they really want it solved |
| 3 | Where | Search demand, sales channels, competitors' distribution | Can we acquire customers at low cost |
| 4 | Why | Competitors' weaknesses, price, switching barriers | Do they choose us |
| 5 | How | Market size, price, cost structure | Do profit and retention hold up |
The five principles are not independent (changing the target changes the problem and the channel too). They are not decided once and for all; revise them repeatedly with the results of market experiments.

"Build from acquisition" = "build from a path by which profitable customers can be acquired repeatedly". In a concept that gathers free users, the link between gathering them and earning revenue matters especially.

## 1. Five principles for thinking about acquisition (arranged for choosing a business)
1. Start from what customers are "already trying to solve". Besides statements that they struggle, look for facts that they spend money, time or people to solve it (they outsource it / a staff member spends hours researching and copying data / they hire people for that job / they pay for an existing service / the current method cannot keep up and they are looking for another). From there, dig into "what they want to achieve that makes them accept that burden" (JTBD). Example: do they want a sales list, or do they want to "decide whom to approach this week"?
2. Narrow customers down to their "situation" and "purchase trigger". Example: "a small IT company with a track record of government contracts, where the sales manager also handles finding tenders and checking participation requirements. They want more orders but lack time to research." Which companies fit / who is in charge / what event creates the need to change / where do they look for a solution at that time. Triggers: contract renewal, a staff member leaving, winning a new project, business expansion, an approaching deadline. The size of the problem and "the reason to act now" go together.
3. Show concretely why customers would change their current method. Convert features into outcomes (AI company information collection → you know which companies to approach this week and why / automatic tender collection → candidate tenders to consider and the conditions to check arrive / workflow automation → less work each time and processing within deadlines / low price → spend less while checking quality). Also look at the adoption burden (difficult setup, data migration, effort to check output, explaining it internally). In early acquisition, showing a real sample fitted to the prospect's own work is a strong approach.
4. Decide how to reach customers together with the product.
   | Customer state | Contact point easiest to test first | What to show |
   |---|---|---|
   | Searching for a solution | Search ads, problem-specific articles, comparison pages | Answers to their questions and concrete outcomes |
   | Target companies can be identified by name | Targeted sales, existing connections | A sample relevant to that company |
   | Consulting experts or business partners | Referrals, partnerships | A target and value the referrer can explain easily |
   | Want to see an explanation or demo before deciding | Videos, seminars, product demos | How it helps in their own work |
   Early on, narrow target companies down from public information and show deliverables relevant to each company (it is easy to see who received it and what they reacted to). Expanding the problems and explanations people reacted to into search articles or videos gives your messaging a basis.
5. Connect the people you gather with the people who pay. Use the free feature → the business need becomes concrete → it reaches a person who can place orders → conditions are discussed → paid transaction. Check whether free-tool users and paying buyers are connected within the same customer's chain of work. If "gather people and something will sell later" remains, that is the hypothesis to test first.

## 2. How far internet research can narrow things down
With public information you can rule out candidates and make the customers and proposals to test concrete. Reactions to your own proposal must be checked by showing it to the people themselves.
| What you want to judge | Evidence to look for online | What that evidence alone does not tell you |
|---|---|---|
| Does the problem exist | Job descriptions in job posts, work procedures, customer posts, case studies | How much it is prioritised now |
| Do they spend to solve it | Contract and order information, paid-service usage cases, outsourcing requests | Whether they would move budget to your proposal |
| What kind of customer | Company track record, organisation, hiring information, people in case studies | Who makes the final decision and who objects |
| Where contact is possible | Search terms, comparison sites, industry associations, competitors' ads | Whether they react to your messaging, acquisition cost |
| What existing means lack | Reviews, FAQs, migration cases, complaints | Whether they would switch if the gap were closed |
| Will it keep making a profit | Public prices, standard work content | Retention, exception handling, support effort, actual gross margin |
Avoid confusing: search volume is not the number of buyers (Keyword Planner's "competition" is competition among advertisers). Competitors' price lists and ads are not proof of profitability. Large development costs or order amounts are separate from willingness to pay you (even if a government agency orders hundreds of millions of yen, how much bidders would pay for a tender-information tool is a separate question). Read evidence narrowly and precisely for "what was actually confirmed".

## 3. A working draft for an existing concept (tender notifications) (a proposal to test; demand not confirmed)
First customer: sales managers at small IT companies with a government track record / Problem: time spent finding tenders and checking participation requirements; they cannot narrow down which to consider / How to find customer candidates: past award results, order records on company websites, descriptions of public-sector business / What to show first: tender candidates that company could consider, with reasons for fit and points to check / Behaviour to confirm: registering their own conditions, putting tenders under consideration, repeated use / Revenue hypothesis after free provision: consultations about IT/AI staff or outsourcing capacity that runs short while pursuing tenders / To verify separately: whether such consultations actually occur, whether they reach someone with ordering authority, whether transaction terms work.
There are two demands to confirm (reduce the burden of finding and considering tenders / secure the people and capacity to execute them). If they connect within the same company, free provision has a revenue reason. Metrics besides registrations: actual tender consideration, capacity consultations, concrete requirement sharing, deals closed, handling effort. Also look at whether the information and explanations repeatedly needed in early individual handling can be extracted and standardised.

## 4. How to run research with AI
- Have AI organise evidence and counter-evidence for each hypothesis rather than mass-generate ideas.
- Make the unit of research "a customer and a situation" (e.g. "what do staff at companies that have just started selling to a particular industry use, and where do they spend time or money, to find target companies and departments?").
- Record evidence and inference separately: source URL and date of the information / who is doing what in what situation / the current way of handling it and the spending or effort that can be confirmed / where contact is possible / what was inferred / facts that do not fit the hypothesis and unverified points. Do not count reposts of the same announcement as separate evidence.
- Test first the uncertainty that drives the next decision: can we reach them → narrow the targets and make contact / is the value understood → company-specific deliverables or demos / do they need it enough to adopt → have them register real data or conditions and use it in their work / will they pay → propose with explicit price, scope and terms / will they continue → is it used in the next work cycle too / is profit left → measure acquisition cost, delivery cost and individual handling time. Treat behaviour — purchase, usage, continuation — as stronger evidence than opinions ("I'm interested" is material for moving to the next test).

## 5. Exit conditions for research
When "target customer / situation in which they struggle / current handling / contact point / deliverable to show / link to revenue" are concrete and the remaining question is "will this person actually use it and pay", move to an experiment. Experiments can also be online (company-specific pages, samples, online meetings, sign-ups, usage logs). Include the running costs of acquisition, trust-building, onboarding support and free provision in the costs.

## 6. Deep dive: what to research and how to evaluate it (from the principles)
The five things you ultimately want to judge: (1) does the problem need solving now (2) who at which company is in that situation (3) can you give them a reason to change from the current method (4) can you contact them repeatedly in a profitable way (5) does usage lead to payment and continuation, leaving a profit. Evaluate "looking attractive" and "having the evidence" separately.

### The unit of research is "the scene where a purchase happens"
Always write at the start of research the sentence "at what kind of company, who, when what happens, needs what outcome, and how they handle it now" (segments should be reachable, measurable, actionable, distinct and large enough).

### Principle (1): "does it need solving" — from behaviour and burden
Items to research: the scene where it occurs / frequency and deadlines / current work (who does what in which order) / where it gets stuck (searching, judging, copying, checking, coordinating) / the impact of leaving it / current handling (adding people, outsourcing, buying software, reducing scope, giving up).
Starting points for searches:
| Source | Search examples | What to extract |
|---|---|---|
| Customer case studies | "operation name" "case study", "tender" "information gathering" "time" | Work before adoption, troubling events, trigger for adoption |
| Company job posts | "sales list" "building" "job", "company name" "hiring" "government" | Job content, department, required skills, headcount |
| Outsourcing requests | "sales list building" "contract work", "operation name" "request" "budget" | Deliverable, volume, deadline, required quality, offered budget |
| Practitioners' articles and posts | "operation name" "manual work", "operation name" "every day", "operation name" "trouble" | Actual procedures, tricks, failures, remaining burden |
| Manuals and FAQs | "operation name" "procedure", "operation name" "Excel" | Steps of current work, checks and exceptions hard to automate |
Evidence that raises priority: it recurs in a specific scene / the impact on deadlines or results is concrete / money, people or time are already being spent / something remains unsolved despite the handling / other companies in the same conditions behave similarly. Evidence that lowers it: the current state is enough / it rarely occurs / improving it barely changes the result.
Strict reading: job post → you learn what they want someone to do, but the intent to replace it with an external service is unverified. Outsourcing request → whether a contract was made at the offered amount is unverified. Effort reduction in a case study → the experience of the featured customer; the whole market is unverified.
Monetising the burden: workload × time per item × internal hourly cost (= an estimate of the burden, not a price they can pay). Do not count the total order value of tenders missed as the loss.

### Principle (2): "who at which company is in that state now"
Conditions for finding customer candidates (tender example): government order record / public-sector business / openings for government sales or project management / expansion into new regions or industries / recruiting external partners. Procurement portals (supplier search, download of award records), gBizINFO (procurement information linked to corporate numbers). Past orders are not evidence of struggling now → overlay current information on business, hiring, outsourcing and operations.
Split people into roles: the person who actually uses it / the person who drives adoption / the person who decides budget and orders / the person who checks adoption conditions. Record the estimated job title and the confirmed decision authority separately.
Reasons to act now: contract renewal is near / a new customer or project was won / staff are short / business or sites are expanding / omissions or delays happened / the terms of the current service changed.
Population caution: collecting only from complaint posts or case studies is biased. Separate research for discovering problems (read in depth to form hypotheses) from research for checking breadth (a group of companies built from conditions such as industry, business and size). When estimating a proportion, align how targets are chosen and the denominator.

### Principle (3): "the reason to change from the current method"
Options: doing it yourself / Excel or internal systems / public information and features / outsourcing / paid services / reducing or dropping the work.
Comparison items: outcome obtained / quality (omissions, errors, staleness, effort to check) / the customer's total burden (fee + setup, checking, operation) / ease of adoption / reasons it is hard to change (integrations, data, contracts, habits, internal approval) / why it is chosen.
Search examples: "service name" "why we adopted", "service name" "switch", "service name" "cancel", "operation name" "automation" "failure", "operation name" "Excel" "operation". Also look for reasons for non-adoption, migration and failure.
Tender notification example: the procurement portal has a notification feature → does the current method cover the ordering agencies they need, are there too many notifications, the burden of judging fit with their own track record, qualifications and staff, keeping track of deadlines and changes, why tenders are found but consideration does not progress. "There is a notification feature" alone does not settle the added value. If evidence that the judging work is heavy accumulates, move to an experiment comparing the current method with your output under the same company conditions.

### Principle (4): "where, and with what words, will it reach them"
A. Identify and contact customers: why they were targeted (which public facts matched) / the relevant person / contact point (existing connections, referrers, public contact windows) / reason for contact (an event that makes telling them now meaningful) / what to show first / the next action you ask for (registering conditions, checking a sample, sharing a problem). Distinguish a connection usable only once from a path repeatable with other companies under the same conditions.
B. Coming from search: by intent ("how to build a sales list" = wants to learn / "how to find a company's department" = wants to get work done / "sales list service price" = comparing outsourcing / "service name alternative" = wants to change / "sales list free" = wants to use it at no cost; does that connect to revenue after free use). Intent is a hypothesis; also check the actual content of the search results. Research table: search term / assumed intent / region and period / estimated search volume / reference bid price / content of search results / page to prepare / action you ask for. If you have not collected the data, leave the numeric fields unverified.
C. Competitors' ads and the flow up to sign-up: Google Ads Transparency Center. Whom and which problem they address, what outcome they promise, what the first action is, how they show cases, samples, prices and track records before sign-up, and where they resolve anxieties and the adoption burden. Even if ads keep running, that does not establish they are profitable.

### Principle (5): "does usage lead to revenue"
Three hypotheses: they want to use the free information service (they register conditions and keep using it in their work) / they want to order staff or outsourcing (actual projects, required skills, budget, start date, external ordering behaviour) / free use leads to orders (there is an orderer inside the using company, and at the right time it moves to consultation and a deal).
Separate buyers, sellers and both (staffing companies and contract developers may be the side that wants to sell). Search examples: "company name" "partner recruitment", "company name" "subcontractor", "company name" "contract work" "recruitment", "company name" "project" "team", "company name" "hiring" "project" (a partner recruitment does not confirm current orders or budget).
Separate the ordering scenes: preparing a bid (looking for external experts or partners for the proposal and delivery team) / after winning (looking for staff or subcontractors to execute a confirmed project). Which one you can create contact at narrows the companies to gather and the design of the free feature.
Look at profitability for the group of companies acquired in the same period (illustrative assumptions): evaluation period 12 months, contribution margin of ¥300,000 per paying company, ¥6,000 per free-using company for acquisition, delivery and pre-sale handling → paid conversion 0.5%: expected ¥1,500 (−¥4,500) / 2%: ¥6,000 (¥0 = break-even before fixed costs) / 5%: ¥15,000 (+¥9,000). The number of free sign-ups alone cannot judge profitability. What to confirm is the paid conversion rate, the profit per deal, and the costs including users who do not convert.

### 7. A worked example of reading public material
A case study of NTT DATA Institute of Management Consulting published on NJSS (a past case published in 2014 and updated in 2023 = not treated as current evidence; a customer interview posted by the seller, not an independent measurement of effect): original method = people checked government websites / burden = collection could take more than an hour and they worried about missing tenders / awareness path = introduced by an in-house researcher / selection behaviour = evaluated coverage and volume of information in a trial, then switched. → Hypotheses you can form: not missing tenders is an important selection reason; comparing real output may help adoption decisions. What you cannot know: whether today's small IT companies have the same demand, how much they would pay, whether they would switch to your product, whether they would order staff or outsourcing after free use. You cannot calculate annual savings from "it could take more than an hour".

### 8. Evaluation method: record "support, counter-evidence, unverified" for each condition for success (recommended over a total score at first)
| Condition needed for success | Supporting evidence | Counter-evidence or other explanation | Unverified | Next check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The problem is strong enough | Concrete burden and handling behaviour | Cases where the current state is enough | Current priority | Check their recent work |
| The target and purchase scene are clear | Named companies and occurring events | The user and the orderer are different | Budget and authority | Check the purchase process |
| There is a reason to change | Conditions existing means cannot meet | Existing means can handle it | Your quality and adoption burden | Sample comparison under the same conditions |
| Customers can be reached | Concrete contact points and people | Contact points are limited and not repeatable | Response rate and acquisition cost | Targeted contact |
| Revenue works | Actual deals and costs | Free users and buyers do not match | Conversion, retention, handling effort | Present the revenue product |
This prevents counter-evidence against an important condition from being buried by high scores elsewhere. If individual handling is needed every time, check fit with "a business that depends little on time and place".
Five points for reading evidence: match with the target / specificity of behaviour (opinion, recruitment, usage, contract, payment) / appropriateness of timing (separate the date of the event from the publication date) / independence (do not count reposts as separate evidence) / selection bias (only success stories, only complaint posts, only a particular seller's customers). "Unverified" does not mean there is no demand.
Fields for AI to keep per piece of evidence: company or subject / problem / event that occurred / observed behaviour / amount, time, frequency / source URL / date of the event and publication date / publisher of the information / inference drawn / counter-evidence / unverified points. Make collection and evaluation separate steps (do not let AI use information it found directly as grounds for its own theory).

### 9. How to decide the next action
| Decision | Condition |
|---|---|
| Continue research | Facts that drive the next decision look confirmable from public information |
| Run a small experiment | Target, problem and proposal are concrete, but reactions and payment are unverified |
| Hold or drop | There is counter-evidence against an essential condition that realistic changes cannot resolve |
| Make it a candidate for more investment | Acquisition, usage, deals and profit have been confirmed to repeat with real customers |
Questions to confirm with customers (recent behaviour rather than future impressions): When did you last do that work? / What happened that made it necessary? / Who handled it, and how? / Where did it take time or money? / Did you consider other methods? / In the end, who decided, and on what conditions? / If you used a real sample in your actual work, what would be missing? "Does it look useful?" or "how much per month?" alone leaves uncertainty. Decide the criteria from the profitability the business needs (do not decide pass/fail by a uniform sign-up rate or number of surveys).
Recommended order: (1) companies where paid deals actually occur and their ordering scenes (buyer, required skills, budget, timing, existing procurement method) → (2) the work those companies do before and after ordering (contact points where a free tool helps) → (3) the burden remaining at that contact point and the path to reach it → (4) confirm both free use and paid deals through actual behaviour. Compare the chance that more research changes the decision with its cost; when the remaining questions can only be answered by customer behaviour, move to showing the real thing (= the exit condition of research).

## 10. Is this enough to fully move a business forward?
It is enough to get started. But the above is "a procedure for researching demand and acquisition and deciding what to test". A business needs three things: "do customers want it", "can you actually deliver it", "does it pay". Execution conditions not yet settled:
| What to make concrete | What to decide | State in which it counts as confirmed |
|---|---|---|
| (1) The first paid deal | Who orders what on what terms, and what payment you receive when | There is a proposal, price, scope and contract terms they can decide on |
| (2) Capability and supply to deliver | Who provides what at the required quality, timing and cost | Confirmed that it can be delivered under real conditions |
| (3) The first experiment | Target, what to show, who is in charge, money and time available, behaviour to observe | You can decide to continue, change or stop depending on the result |
| (4) Profit and operations | Who handles acquisition through delivery, billing, payment and inquiries, and at what cost | Including the founder's work in costs, profit and the cash burden until payment are understood |
(2) supply is especially easy to miss: buyers have concrete orders, and there is a supply side that can meet those conditions. Under what contract form you participate and what you take on (licences and contract preparation are unverified).
Judging "progress" at each stage: demand verification (they have the problem and act to solve it) → first-deal verification (contract under concrete terms, deliver value and receive payment) → repeatability verification (it works with another similar customer) → profitability verification (profit remains including costs for customers who did not buy) → operations verification (it can be repeated at the needed quality by people other than you). The first deal is evidence that a transaction can happen; evidence of stable revenue comes from repetition. If the founder carries sales, coordination and quality checks every time, confirm that profit remains after handing them over.
